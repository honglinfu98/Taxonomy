\label{sec:exp:rq1}

\paragraph{What the corpus supports.}
The run produced 93 term types with 317 values and 212 aliases
(\cref{tab:taska}). Technology dimensions are the largest group, because the
blockchain taxonomy of Tasca and Tessone contributes architectural dimensions
that no legal instrument uses, such as the fee regime and the
interoperability of a ledger. Asset and actor dimensions are the most widely
shared. An asset term type is grounded in 4.5 documents on average, and
the status of an actor as a service provider is grounded in 13 of the 24
documents. Twenty
term types rest on a single document. Most of these come from the two academic
taxonomies, which draw distinctions the law does not yet make, such as the
mechanism by which yield reaches a holder, or from instruments with a
specialised vocabulary, such as the serious-crime threshold of the UN
cybercrime convention. Thirty-one term types are grounded in both a legal
instrument and an academic paper. Every document is cited by at least one term
type; the FATF guidance of 2021 is cited most, by 27, and the DLT-Corpus paper
least, by one. The model's own coverage notes explain the latter. That paper's sections
describe data sources and metrics rather than classification criteria.

Sixty-nine term types carry a note recording a disagreement between sources.
The reference-asset dimension is typical. It is grounded in seven documents
across four jurisdictions and takes five values, and its note records that
\src{MiCA} splits stablecoins by whether they reference one official currency
or anything else, that the \src{GENIUS Act} and the UK regulations admit only
fiat references, and that \src{FATF} and the \src{FSB} apply their standards
whatever the reference. The fungibility dimension records that fungibility is
an element of the UK and US definitions of the asset class but not of the FATF
or \src{MiCA} definitions. These notes are the raw material for the
jurisdiction-indexed nodes of \cref{sec:method:hierarchy}.

\paragraph{Faithfulness.}
Of the 480 citations, 463 matched the cited section verbatim under
normalisation, 7 were relabelled, and 10 were rejected, a rejection rate of
2.1\%. The manifest did what we built it for. One cited label did not exist, a
subsection heading inside the FinCEN guidance that the model used in place of
the enclosing section's label, and six named a real but wrong section of the
right document (three cite the FATF glossary for passages in its Part Two, three
shorten a long label of the crypto-asset taxonomy). The verifier located the
quote in each of these seven cases. Wrong or non-existent labels therefore fell
from 9\% of citations in the run without the manifest to 1.5\%, and the number
of term types rose from 65 to 93.

All ten rejections are quotes the verifier could not find. Two share no
five-word sequence with the cited section. The other eight are recognisable
compressions of the source. The model drops enumerated items or connectives
from a statutory list, as in ``'blockchain' means any technology (i) where data
is shared across a network'', or it reproduces most of a sentence and then
rephrases its end. These quotes point at the right passage but are not
verbatim, so we keep them in the output as unverified rather than repairing
them. Every term type keeps at least one verified citation, but five values
were supported only by a rejected quote and are therefore unverified.

Verification is a necessary check, not a sufficient one. A verbatim quote can
still fail to support the dimension it is attached to, and our verifier does
not read for relevance. Whether the 470 verified citations do support their
term types is the question the expert annotation of \cref{sec:exp:setup} is
designed to answer, and we have not run it yet.
