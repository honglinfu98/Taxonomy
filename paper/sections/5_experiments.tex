\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:exp}

We answer seven research questions.
\textbf{RQ1}: How many classification dimensions does the corpus support, how
widely is each shared across sources, and how faithful are the citations?
\textbf{RQ2}: What hierarchy do the term types form, how confident and how
grounded are its edges, and how does it change as weak edges are hidden?
\textbf{RQ3}: Does the DAG serve as a label space for Web content, and does
the content reveal where it must grow?
\textbf{RQ4}: Does the DAG index retrieve tagged content faster than a flat
tag table, and does facet gain navigate to a target item?
\textbf{RQ5}: How does the ontology compare with expert taxonomies and LLM
baselines given the same corpus? \textbf{RQ6}: How much do the manifest,
grounding strategy and model matter? \textbf{RQ7}: Does the ontology improve a
downstream classification of Web-sourced token descriptions? RQ1 to RQ4 and
the manifest part of RQ6 are answered below; the rest are framed.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:exp:setup}

\textbf{Datasets.} \corpus is described in \cref{tab:corpus}. No benchmark
exists for term type induction, so \corpus is both the input and, via its
citations, the ground truth for faithfulness. For RQ3 the
content is DLT-Corpus~\cite{hernandez2026dltcorpus}, 37,440 scientific papers
and 49,023 USPTO patents, of which we keep English items whose title or
abstract names a DLT term (40\% of papers, 49\% of patents) and draw disjoint
batches: a pilot of 150 papers and 150 patents, then a tenth of each
collection (3,744 papers, 4,902 patents); an item is the title and abstract
capped at 450 words. For RQ4 the index holds the tagged items of both batches. \todo{Held-out set and expert gold set for RQ5 and RQ7; contamination note.}

\textbf{Baselines.} \todo{Expert taxonomies; zero-shot LLM without the corpus; chunked RAG and
RAPTOR over it; TnT-LLM and LLMs4OL-style term typing; same corpus, model and
schema for all.}

\textbf{Metrics.} For faithfulness we report the share of citations whose quote
the verifier finds in the cited document (exact or relabelled), the share
relabelled, and the rejection rate; smaller rejection is better. For coverage we
report the number of term types, values and aliases, the number of documents
grounding each term type, and the number of term types with a recorded
conflict. For the hierarchy we report the structural counts and the objectives of
\cref{sec:method:hierarchy}; for retrieval, latency against a flat scan with
identical results and the steps and bits a navigation needs to reach $k$
items. \todo{For RQ5 add precision and recall of term
types against the expert gold set, and coverage of each expert taxonomy's
dimensions; for RQ7 add macro-F$_1$ of the downstream classifier.}

\textbf{Implementation.} Both tasks use \texttt{claude-haiku-5-5} called
through the Anthropic Messages API at effort \texttt{high} with adaptive
thinking disabled; the corpus sits behind a one-hour prompt-cache breakpoint.
Task~A is one streamed call with a 120,000-token output limit; Task~B is 93
calls with $k{=}6$, four in parallel; Task~C runs at effort \texttt{medium}
with six items in flight. All runs are API calls from a laptop; no GPU is
involved. The retrieval engine is pure Python over integer bitsets and is
timed on one core of the same laptop. We report a single run. \todo{Add a second run with the same prompt

\label{sec:exp:rq1}

  Confl.\ counts term types whose notes record a disagreement between sources;
  Verif.\ is the share of citations whose quote the verifier found in the cited
  document, exact or relabelled. Single run.}
  \label{tab:taska}
  \scriptsize
  \input{tables/task_a_summary}
\end{table}


\textbf{What the corpus supports.} The run produced 93 term types with 317
values and 212 aliases (\cref{tab:taska}). We make the
following observations. (1) Technology dimensions are the largest group,
because the blockchain taxonomy of Tasca and Tessone contributes architectural
dimensions that no legal instrument uses, such as the fee regime and the
interoperability of a ledger. (2) Asset and actor dimensions are the most
widely shared: an asset term type is grounded in 4.5 documents on average,
and service-provider status in 13 of the 24. (3) Twenty term types rest on a single document, mostly distinctions the
academic taxonomies draw and the law does not yet make. Every document is
cited by at least one term type, the 2021 FATF guidance most, by 27, and the
DLT-Corpus paper least, by one.


Sixty-nine term types carry a note recording a disagreement between sources.
\Cref{tab:case} (\cref{app:results}) shows the reference-asset dimension of
\cref{fig:one} as returned: five values, seven grounding documents across four
jurisdictions, and a note recording the jurisdictional split.


\textbf{Faithfulness.} Of the 480 citations, 463 matched the cited section
verbatim under normalisation, 7 were relabelled, and 10 were rejected, a
rejection rate of 2.1\% (\cref{tab:taska}, last column), from 95.1\% for actor
dimensions to 100\% for asset and activity dimensions. One cited label did not exist, a subsection heading inside
the FinCEN guidance, and six named a wrong section of the right document; the
verifier located the quote in all seven. \Cref{tab:errors} in \cref{app:results} lists the ten rejections with a
diagnosis. Two
quotes share no five-word sequence with the cited section. Three compress a
definition, two drop the enumerated items of a statutory list, as in
``'blockchain' means any technology (i) where data is shared across a
network'', and three reproduce most of a sentence and then rephrase its end.
They point at the right passage but are not verbatim, so we keep them as
unverified. Every term type keeps at
least one verified citation, but five values were supported only by a rejected
quote and are therefore unverified.

Verification checks existence, not relevance: whether the 470 verified
citations support their term types is the question for the expert annotation
planned in \cref{sec:exp:setup}, which we have not run. The run is one call of
302 seconds with 85,729 output tokens and 235,298 cached input tokens, at most
\$0.33 at list price.

\subsection{RQ2: The Is-a Hierarchy}
\label{sec:exp:rq2}

  Bits/desc.\ is the information gain of a random root-to-leaf descent;
  E[depth] its expected length; AUC the area under the reach-a-leaf curve (1 when
  every root is a leaf); Eff.\,lv.\ is the effective number of leaves
  $2^{\entropy(r)}$ for the leaf-reach distribution $r$. Single run.}
  \label{tab:taskb}
  \scriptsize
  \input{tables/task_b_summary}
\end{table}


\textbf{Structure.} The 93 calls returned \BWeighted\ weighted candidate edges,
of which consolidation kept \BEdges\ and dropped \BDropped\ for closing a cycle,
with \BTies\ exact tie (\cref{tab:taskb}). We make the following observations.
(1) Every category closes under a single root, the crypto-asset definitional
criteria for assets, the governance model for technologies, service-provider
status for actors, the service type for activities, and AML applicability for
obligations; the model was free to return \emph{none} for any node and did so
with median mass 0.13. (2) The DAG is deep and multi-parented: a node keeps
\BMeanParents\ parents on average and up to \BMaxParents, the technology category
reaches depth 14, and a random descent resolves \BBits\ bits before ending at
one of \BEffLeaves\ effective leaves. (3) In the asset category (\cref{fig:tree}, \cref{app:results}) the reference-asset dimension
specialises the legal classification and carries the stablecoin class,
redemption mechanism and tokenisation form beneath it, but second-choice
parents carry nearly as much mass: \emph{legal classification} and
\emph{stablecoin regulatory class} both received 0.32 as parents of
\emph{reference asset}, and \BMutual\ node pairs were each proposed as the
other's parent. The weights are coarse, with a
median top weight of \BMedianTop, so we read them as rankings rather than
calibrated probabilities. The \BCalls\ calls took \BSeconds\ seconds and read
\BCacheTokens\ million cached tokens, at most \$11.7 before the cache-read
discount. \todo{Exact billing figures; baseline and frontier-model cost.}


\textbf{Sensitivity to the threshold.} \Cref{fig:spectrum} (\cref{app:results}) sweeps a display
threshold over the consolidated DAG. Hiding edges below 0.2 halves the edge
count and roughly halves the bits per descent; at 0.4 a third of the nodes are
roots and a descent resolves half a bit; above 0.6 the graph is a forest of
near-singletons.

\textbf{Faithfulness of edges.} The entries carry \BCites\ citations, of which
\BVerified\ (\BVerifiedPct\%) verify, \BRelabelled\ after relabelling, and
\BRejected\ are rejected, a higher rejection rate than in Task~A. Only
\BEntriesCited\ of the \BWeighted\ entries cite anything, which the prompt
allows for low-probability entries, and \BTopNoCite\ top entries cite nothing,
which it does not. Rejected quotes are mostly short phrases from long sections, such as
``Wrapped tokens''.

\subsection{RQ3: The DAG as a Label Space}
\label{sec:exp:rq3}

  catalogue version in force, measures the DAG on it, discovers candidates at
  the target nodes and consolidates the accepted ones into the next version.
  Evid.\ is the share of evidence spans found verbatim in the item; Nodes used
  counts nodes that received at least one item; USD and minutes cover tagging,
  discovery and consolidation at list price with eight workers.}
  \label{tab:evolution}
  \scriptsize
  \input{tables/evolution}
\end{table*}

\textbf{Tagging.} \Cref{tab:evolution} summarises both iterations. The pilot
batch of \CItems\ items received \CTags\ tags, \CTagsPerItem\ per item, and the
second batch of \TItems\ items, a tenth of the on-topic collection, received
\TTags\ tags, \TTagsPerItem\ per item; \CEvidencePct\% and \TEvidencePct\% of
the evidence spans were found verbatim in the item, and \CRejected\ and
\TRejected\ tags were rejected for unknown identifiers. We make the following
observations. (1) The content exercises the technical half of the DAG: in the
second batch \TCovTech\ items carry a technology tag and \TCovAsset\ an asset
tag, against \TCovActor\ for actors, \TCovActivity\ for activities and
\TCovOblig\ for obligations. The legal nodes are dormant or collapsed on one value; testing them needs
regulatory content. (2) The dimensions discovered in
iteration 1 are among the most used in iteration 2: ledger purpose was tagged
on \TLedgerPurpose\ items with a value entropy of \TEntLedgerPurpose\ bits,
smart-contract usage domain on \TUsageDomain, identity binding on \TIdentity\
and the privacy-mechanism family on \TPrivacy\ with \TEntPrivacy\ bits, so
they discriminate the content rather than absorbing it. (3) Tagging cost
\$\TCostPerItem\ per item; the whole on-topic collection of \OnTopicK\
thousand documents would cost about \$\ProjCost\ and \ProjHours\ hours.

\textbf{Measurement.} \Cref{tab:objectives} (\cref{app:results}) compares, for each version, the
objectives from the model's is-a weights with those measured from the tags. A
content-driven descent resolves about seven bits against under five from the
prior and ends at more effective leaves: the content goes deeper and spreads
wider than the prior weights suggested. Collapsed nodes grow with the batch,
\TCollapsed\ in iteration 2, led by the DLT data structure, 96\% of 2,377
items on ``blockchain''; they are also the largest residual pools, which is
where discovery is pointed.

\textbf{Discovery and evolution.} Iteration 1 found \MTargets\ targets and
accepted \MAccepted\ of \MCandidates\ candidates; iteration 2 found \TTargets\
targets and accepted \TAccepted\ of \TCandidates, producing version 2 with
\VTwoNodes\ term types, \VTwoEdges\ edges and \VTwoValues\ values
(\cref{tab:accepted}, \cref{app:results}). The additions are dimensions a legal corpus would not
contain, such as the cryptographic family behind a privacy guarantee, and
several iteration-2 values refine iteration-1 nodes. Two failures are visible:
two application-domain dimensions proposed under different smart-contract
nodes in iteration 1 ask the same question and were not merged by
consolidation; and with raw support counts, capped by the forty items shown,
only 10 of 76 candidates cleared the gain test against residual pools of
thousands in iteration 2, which is why support is extrapolated to the residual
(21 of 81 accepted).

The tags have no gold labels yet, so verified evidence is the only quality
signal we report; an evidence span can exist without the tag being right.

\subsection{RQ4: Retrieval over the DAG}
\label{sec:exp:rq4}

\begin{table}[t]
  \centering
  \caption{Query latency on the index of \RItems\ items (version 2), per
  query on one core, best of \RRepeat\ runs for the bitset engine and a single
  run for the flat scan over the tag table. Hits is the mean result size.
  Every bitset result was identical to the scan's.}
  \label{tab:retrieval}
  \scriptsize
  \input{tables/retrieval}
\end{table}

\textbf{Index.} The on-topic, tagged items of both batches, \RItems\ with
\RTags\ tags, are placed on version 2, where \RNodesTagged\ of \RNodes\ nodes
and \RPairs\ node--value pairs hold items; the index builds in \RBuildSec\
seconds and occupies \RIndexMB~MB. \Cref{tab:retrieval} times eight workloads,
\RQueries\ queries drawn from the nodes and values that hold items, against a
flat scan that tests one predicate per item with precomputed ancestor sets.
(1) Every workload runs in \RLatMin\ to \RLatMax\ microseconds against
\RFlatMinMs\ to \RFlatMaxMs\ milliseconds for the scan, \RSpeedMin\ to
\RSpeedMax\ times faster, with \RMismatches\ mismatches in \RQueries\ results.
(2) Navigation by facet gain works. For \RTargets\ random target items we start
from the whole collection and take the option of the offered facet that
contains the target until at most $k{=}\RK$ items remain or 15 steps are
spent; the ideal is \RIdealBits\ bits. The highest-narrowing facet resolves
\RGainBits\ bits in \RGainSteps\ steps and reaches $k$ for \RGainReached\% of
the targets, against \RRandBits\ bits in \RRandSteps\ steps and
\RRandReached\% for a random facet; the mean exceeds the ideal because the last
step often narrows below $k$, while the targets that miss $k$ leave the mean
final set at \RGainFinal\ items. (3) The failure is
inherited from the edge weights: through light is-a edges the subtree of the
asset root reaches \RTopClosurePct\% of the items, so a bare subtree query on
a high node returns most of the collection. Restricting the subtree to path strength $\ge 0.3$ cuts the mean
hit count from 1,474 to 579 at twice the latency, and the candidate edges
added at weight one will tighten once the is-a query re-estimates them.
Ranking uses the tagger's confidence; there are no relevance judgements yet. \todo{Relevance judgements
